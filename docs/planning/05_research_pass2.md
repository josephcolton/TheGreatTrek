## 1. What this changes in the design

1. **The route is longer than we thought:** about **540 miles in a straight line**, so roughly **650–700 miles on the ground** (§2). Taking ~650 mi as the game length fits the history: the Uys party took about 87 days from Grahamstown to the Sand River.
2. **Prices now have real anchors:** the Graham's Town market (1831–32), Harris (1836), and Backhouse (1838–39). An ox went from **£1 5s before the war** to **about 45 Rds (£3 7s 6d)**, "considered high", by 1838 (§4).
3. **Sell meal by the *muid*** (about 180 lb), the unit people used then. It teaches a period word and makes the prices match sources directly.
4. **Servants' wages:** about **10 Rds (15s) a month**, found in two separate sources (§4.3).
5. **Price shocks:** the frontier war (sourced), Vegkop losses and the cattle taken at Mosega (sourced), and cheap farms sold by trekkers (sourced example). **No source links the 1837 financial panic to the Cape**, so drop it unless one turns up (§5).
6. **Facts checked:** quagga on the route (Backhouse saw a troop near Colesberg in 1839); horse-sickness season; lungsickness only from 1853; Ordinance 50 (§7).
7. **Two route facts to know (not re-opening the decision):** Retief's party crossed the Orange at **Buffelsvlei** (later Aliwal North), east of our Colesberg route, and the Uys party joined the main laager at the **Sand River on 29 June 1837**. Our Colesberg route is still a real road north, but a History note should say that other parties crossed further east.

## 2. Route and distances

Straight-line distances between the modern places, worked out from their coordinates. Ox-wagon tracks wound around hills and between water points, so on-the-ground distance is taken as **×1.25–1.3** of the straight line **[estimate]**.

| Leg | Straight line | On the ground (×1.25) |
|---|---|---|
| Grahamstown → Cradock | 95 mi | ~120 mi |
| Cradock → Colesberg | 104 mi | ~130 mi |
| Colesberg → Orange drift (Norvalspont area) | 22 mi | ~28 mi |
| Orange drift → Thaba Nchu | 129 mi | ~160 mi |
| (detour via Philippolis) | | +~23 mi |
| Thaba Nchu → Vet River (Winburg area) | 49 mi | ~60 mi |
| Vet River → Sand River (Ventersburg area) | 31 mi | ~40 mi |
| Sand River → Vaal drift (Parys area) | 84 mi | ~105 mi |
| Vaal drift → Mooi River (Potchefstroom) | 25 mi | ~30 mi |
| **Total** | **539 mi** | **~675 mi** |

The modern places stand in for 1837 crossing points; which drift a given party used varied.

**Checking against history.** The Uys party received the Bible at Grahamstown in April 1837 **[sourced]** and reached the Sand River laager on 29 June 1837 **[sourced: Wikipedia, Piet Uys]**. That is about 87 days for ~540 mi on the ground, or **~6.2 mi/day overall including rest days**. This matches the sourced "about 10 km/day with herds". **Game setting:** ~650 mi; normal pace 8–9 mi on a travel day, giving ~6–7 mi/day with Sundays and stops. A full trip is then ~100–110 days, arriving in **July**, in the Highveld winter.

**Other parties' crossings:** Retief's group crossed the Orange at Buffelsvlei in March 1837 **[sourced, Afrikaans Retief biography; verify]**. Buffelsvlei was the farm where Aliwal North was later built. Sanddrift, about 18 km upstream of Aliwal North, is shown crossing "the Great River" on a Voortrekker Monument panel **[sourced]**. The trekkers called the Orange the *Groot Rivier* (Great River) **[sourced]**. That is worth putting in the glossary.

## 8. How the families came to be in South Africa

### 8.1 The Dutch side: background

- **1652:** the Dutch East India Company (VOC) sent Jan van Riebeeck to build a fort and gardens at Table Bay to supply its ships. It was meant as a supply station, not a colony.
- **1657:** the Company let some of its men leave service and farm as *free burghers* along the Liesbeeck River. The supply station became a colony.
- **From 1688:** French Huguenot refugees arrived, about one-sixth of the free burghers, mostly at Drakenstein. Many German soldiers and sailors of the Company also settled and married into Dutch families.
- **1707:** Hendrik Biebouw (spelled Bibault in some sources) said "'K ben een Africaander", the earliest known claim to be an African-born colonist.
- **1700s:** the *trekboers* moved north and east with their herds, past the Company's limits. Graaff-Reinet was founded in 1786 to bring them back under control. By 1800 they had reached the Great Fish River, and frontier wars with the Xhosa had begun. Khoikhoi and San lost land and were drawn into farm labour. Many colonists held enslaved people.
- **1795 and 1806:** Britain took the Cape. Slagtersnek (1815) left a bitter memory. Ordinance 50 (1828) and emancipation (1834) changed farm labour. The Sixth Frontier War (1834–35) and the giving back of Queen Adelaide Province (1836) left frontier farmers feeling unprotected.

### 8.2 The English side: background

- **1820:** about 4,000–4,500 British settlers came in about 60 parties on some 21–28 ships, landing at Algoa Bay from **about 9–10 April 1820** (the *Chapman* first, then the *Nautilus* and others). They were placed in the Zuurveld, renamed **Albany**, as a buffer on the frontier.
- **1820s:** wheat crops failed again and again with **rust** (1820–23 and worse in 1826). Families lived on maize, pumpkins and potatoes, and many moved to Graham's Town as artisans and traders. Labour and craftsmen were in short supply. The frontier trade in hides, ivory, tallow and butter grew quickly.
- **1834–35:** the Sixth Frontier War hit Albany hard.

### 8.3 Draft origin stories (2–3 paragraphs each)

These are **fictional families** written over the real history above. Names in *italics* are defaults that the player can change, using the lists in §9.

**Dutch 1: The Frontier Farmer (*Grensboer*). Main character *Gerrit Badenhorst*.**
> Gerrit's first ancestor at the Cape was a German soldier of the Company, who took his discharge in 1712 to farm as a free burgher and married the daughter of a Huguenot family at Drakenstein. Each generation after moved further east with the cattle, looking for grass and water, until Gerrit's grandfather took out a loan farm in the Graaff-Reinet district in the 1780s.
>
> Gerrit grew up on the Bushmans River with his father's herds and the Khoikhoi families who worked them. In the war of 1834–35 the farm was raided, the cattle were driven off, and the homestead burned. The new province beyond the Kei was meant to make the frontier safe. When London gave it back in 1836, Gerrit decided the Colony would never protect his family.
>
> Now he has sold the farm for less than it was worth. With his wife Susanna, their children, old Klaas the voorloper and Klaas's family, he has come to Grahamstown, where the Uys party is gathering to go north.

**Dutch 2: The Blacksmith (*Smid*). Main character *Hendrik Kotzé*.**
> The Kotzés came to the Cape as smiths: Hendrik's great-grandfather mended the Company's wagons at the Castle in Cape Town. The family moved inland with the farmers they served, and Hendrik learned the trade at his father's forge in Uitenhage, shoeing horses and putting iron tyres on wagon wheels.
>
> Hendrik had customers, not cattle. But his customers were leaving one by one, selling up and heading for the Orange River, and they begged him to come: a trek without a smith, they said, would leave its wagons broken in the veld. He loaded his anvil, bellows and iron onto the wagon, though the weight worries him.

**Dutch 3: The Hunter (*Jagter*). Main character *Barend du Plessis*.**
> The du Plessis family were Huguenots who fled France for their faith and reached the Cape in 1688. Barend's people kept the French name but lost the language within two generations. Like many trekboer families they lived from cattle and the gun, and Barend has spent more of his life on horseback in the veld than at home.
>
> He knows the country beyond the Orange from hunting trips: the game herds, the springs, and the Griqua and Barolong he traded with for ivory and skins. Now hunting is not just a living but a way to feed a family on the road, and the trek leaders want men who know the land ahead. His wife thinks he loves the veld more than the farm, and she may be right.

**Dutch 4: The Predikant's Household. Main character *Johanna Marais* (or her husband *Daniël*).**
> No minister of the Dutch Reformed Church would leave the Colony with the trekkers, so the families who went north had to find their own spiritual leaders **[recall, verify]**. Daniël Marais is not an ordained minister but a teacher and lay preacher, the son of a Huguenot family from the Drakenstein valley who came east as a schoolmaster on frontier farms.
>
> His wife Johanna keeps a diary, as some trekker women did. She writes down the hymns sung at night, the illnesses, the births and the graves. The family is poor in money and has few men to stand guard, but the trekkers ask them to read the Bible, nurse the sick, and keep the children's schooling going.
>
> *(This family is **inspired by** the role of Erasmus and Susanna Smit; it does not portray them.)*

**English 1: The 1820 Settlers (the *Ashworths*). Main character *Thomas Ashworth*.**
> In early 1820 Thomas Ashworth, a weaver whose trade the new mills had ruined, sailed from England with his wife Mary and their small children in one of the settler parties. After about three months at sea they landed on the beach at Algoa Bay in April 1820. Ox wagons hired from Dutch farmers then carried them inland to a plot in the Zuurveld, now called Albany.
>
> Wheat rust ruined the crops year after year. The family lived on pumpkins and maize, and Thomas learned to farm cattle, ride, shoot, and speak the Dutch of the frontier. The war of 1834–35 burned their house. Their son William, born at the Cape, knows no other home.
>
> In April 1837 the Ashworths stand in Grahamstown among the settlers giving a Bible to the departing trekkers. William has decided to go north with a wagon of goods, and his parents have decided not to let him go alone.

**English 2: The Trader. Main character *Samuel Pascoe*.**
> Samuel Pascoe came out with his father's party as a boy of twelve, from Cornwall. The family's grant failed like so many others, and by 1825 his father had given up farming to work as a carpenter in Graham's Town, where tradesmen were scarce.
>
> Samuel grew up at the daily market, among wagons from the far frontier loaded with ivory, hides and skins. He worked for a merchant and made his first trading journeys beyond the colonial boundary. Now hundreds of trekker families are heading north, and they will need powder, coffee, cloth and iron. Samuel has loaded a heavy wagon on credit, and he is going with them.

**English 3: The Hunter-Traveller. Main character *Edward Fenwick*.**
> Edward Fenwick was eight when his family came out with the 1820 settlers. While his elder brothers took on the farm, Edward was sent to school in Cape Town and came back with a taste for natural history and an old fowling-piece.
>
> He has read about the great game herds north of the Orange, and in 1836 he met the English officer Captain Harris outfitting in Graham's Town for a hunting journey to Mzilikazi's country. Edward means to see that country himself. He is going north with a light wagon, two horses and too few oxen, a hunter rather than a settler.
>
> *(Harris is a historical figure and may appear as himself; the meeting is invented.)*

### 8.4 Servants and companions

Each family's servants get a short origin too, in their own voice where possible:
- a **Khoikhoi** voorloper whose people herded cattle in the same valleys before the trekboers came, and who, since 1828, is free to work for whom he chooses;
- a **formerly enslaved** woman freed in 1834 (still bound as an "apprentice" until 1838), now paid wages, whose grandparents were brought from the East Indies or Mozambique;
- an **Mfengu** herder who came into the Colony during the 1835 war and works under a contract that gives him fewer rights than the Khoikhoi.

**[sourced]** Slavery ended in law at the Cape on **1 December 1834**; the freed people were then bound to their former owners as unpaid "apprentices" until **1 December 1838** (Iziko Slave Lodge; Ekama et al., AEHN working paper 53, 2020). So in April 1837 such a woman would **still be an apprentice**, not a free wage-earner: she could travel with a family only as an apprentice of her former owner, or if she had bought her freedom. The servant stories must say which.

## 9. Name lists

These names are used together at random, or chosen by the player. Rules:
- They should sound right for the period.
- **Avoid the surnames of trek leaders and famous people**: Uys, Retief, Potgieter, Maritz, Cilliers, Pretorius, Tregardt/Trichardt, Steenkamp, Smit, Kruger, van Rensburg, Bezuidenhout, Liebenberg, Erasmus, Oosthuizen, and the settler-leaders Philipps, Pringle, Bowker, Godlonton, Biggar.

## 10. Event seeds from Harris (1836–37), same road and years

All **[sourced: Harris, *Wild Sports*, 1839]**:
1. **Wagon upset near Graham's Town:** the baggage wagon fell into a deep hole by the road; the upper works smashed, the goods mostly safe.
2. **Quicksand:** his horse sank in quicksand crossing the Little Fish River and nearly drowned.
3. **Horses go home:** two horses vanished in the night, thought to have gone back to the grazing where they were bred.
4. **Careless herders:** while the herders dozed, the cattle strayed; two oxen died and the rest lost condition.
5. **Oxen dropping:** "three more of our oxen dropped down on the road" near the colonial border.
6. **Mirage:** thirsty oxen ran for a "lake" that was only salt crust on the ground.
7. **The Orange crossing:** the water was so deep they built raised platforms inside the wagons to keep the goods dry.
8. **Thirst:** oxen bellowing for water after a dry march; a stampede into a muddy pool.
9. **Night thieves:** hyenas, drawn by the smell of mutton, ate gear left out.
10. **Dogs' paws:** the dogs' feet were blistered raw by the hot ground and were treated with the tar-and-fat axle grease; the dogs came and held out a paw for more.
11. **Storm:** reeds thrashing in a storm frightened the cattle so that "they thrice broke loose".
12. **News on the road:** reports that the wagons of a hunting farmer named Erasmus had been captured on the Vaal River, before Vegkop. (This is a real person and event: use it only as news, framed carefully.)
13. **Gunpowder bargain:** people would sell horses only for gunpowder, which was illegal to trade (§7.4 of the design).

From Backhouse (1838–39): a **troop of quaggas with foals at sunrise**; **oxen straying overnight after smelling grass far off**; Boers who **kept their "old price"** while others raised theirs (a good choice for the player when selling).

### 14.3 Tregardt's diary

The 1938 edition (Preller, ed., *Dagboek van Louis Trichardt 1836–1838*, Nasionale Pers) is free on DBNL: https://www.dbnl.org/tekst/tric015dagb01_01/ . It covers Tregardt's party in the far north in 1836–38, not our route, so it is used for **daily life**, not for places:
- **Sunday:** see §7. Tregardt kept it; others did not always.
- **Animals:** horses died of "de paarde siekte" (horse-sickness); cattle died of a "stop siekte"; he counted his cattle often (454 head in Sept 1837). Used for the horse-sickness event.
- **Powder and lead:** by 1837–38 the party's powder was down to about one keg and they melted tin and copper for bullets (Preller's introduction). Used for the "lead runs short" event.
- **Daily work:** the diary is full of wheel bands burnt and reset, hides pegged out, soap boiled, sheep slaughtered, hunting for eland and quagga, and lost cattle searched for. Good material for small road events.
- **Births and deaths** are recorded plainly (twins born and one dying days later, Oct 1836). The game keeps such things in text and non-graphic.
