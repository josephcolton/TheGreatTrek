# The Great Trek: Historical Research

The history behind the game: the sourced facts, prices, dates, distances, people and places it is built on, with links. The game's data files and its "history behind this" notes cite this document by section (for example "research.md section 7.2"). How the game uses these facts is in `design.md`.

---

## 1. How to read this

Facts carry one of these labels:

- **[sourced]**: found in the linked source.
- **[conflict]**: sources disagree; the game says so in its history notes.
- **[recall, verify]**: general knowledge not yet checked against a source. Don't put these in event text as fact.
- **[estimate]**: worked out from sourced figures, with the working shown.
- **[invented]**: made up for play, and marked as such in the data.

Full links are in section 24.

## 2. The Great Trek: overview

- **Dates:** c. 1835 to the early or mid-1840s. **[sourced]**
- **Numbers:** about 12,000–14,000 Boers. One source estimates a roughly equal number of Khoikhoi and Black servants travelled with them. **[sourced/conflict]**
- **Two main directions:** north across the Orange River onto the Highveld, or north-east towards Natal. **[sourced]**
- **Main parties:**
  - **Van Rensburg** (49 people): headed north and was killed (section 16.1). **[sourced]**
  - **Louis Tregardt (Trichardt):** Soutpansberg, then Delagoa Bay, arriving 13 Apr 1838. About half the party died, mostly of malaria, including Tregardt and his wife Martha (section 16). **[sourced]**
  - **Hendrik Potgieter:** attacked by the Ndebele (Mzilikazi) at the **Battle of Vegkop**, Oct 1836 (16th or 20th) **[conflict]**. Afterwards Moroka of the Barolong sent oxen to bring the stranded trek to **Thaba Nchu**. **[sourced]**
  - **Gert Maritz, Piet Retief, Piet Uys, Sarel Cilliers.** Retief's manifesto was published in the *Graham's Town Journal* in Jan 1837. **[sourced]**
- **Natal:** Retief camped at **Kerkenberg** with 54 wagons (5 Oct 1837) and went down the Drakensberg by **Retief's Pass**. Retief and his party were killed at Dingane's capital uMgungundlovu (Feb 1838), followed by the attacks on the camps (Bloukrans/Weenen) and the Battle of **Blood River / Ncome** (16 Dec 1838). Then came the Republic of Natalia, the British siege of Port Natal, and the Battle of Congella (1842). **[sourced]** (Details in section 17.)
- **Reasons given for the trek:**
  - Retief's manifesto lists labour and "vagrancy" grievances, frontier insecurity, and British rule.
  - The emancipation of the enslaved (effective 1834–38) and the compensation complaints. How much these mattered is debated among historians. **[sourced]**

## 3. Period sources

**Diaries and journals of the trekkers**
- **Louis Tregardt's diary (1836–38).** The only trek leader who kept a diary; the best source for daily life, illness, oxen and trade. The 1938 edition (Preller, ed., *Dagboek van Louis Trichardt 1836–1838*) is free on DBNL. English edition translated by Jackie Grobler. What the game takes from it is in section 10.2.
- **Anna Steenkamp's journal** (Retief's niece). A family chronicle of the Trek, often quoted on why families left. Studied in A.W.G. Raath (NWU, 2016/17).
- **Susanna Smit's spiritual diary.** The wife of predikant Erasmus Smit; over 1000 pages, held in the Natal Archives, transcribed in M. Puddu (Wits MA, 1996). The background for the predikant's household (section 12.3).
- **Sarel Cilliers.** Preacher, and the man behind the Blood River vow.
- **The *Graham's Town Journal*** (1830s): printed Retief's manifesto.

**Travellers on the same road in the same years** (public domain, full text on archive.org)
- **W. Cornwallis Harris**, *The Wild Sports of Southern Africa* (1839), first printed as *Narrative of an Expedition…* (Bombay, 1838). He outfitted at Grahamstown in 1836, crossed the Orange, visited Mzilikazi (he was at the king's headquarters when news came that trekkers were crossing the Vaal), met the Ndebele force coming back from Vegkop, and was back at the Cape in Jan 1837. The best English-side source for the northern route in these years. His *Portraits of the Game and Wild Animals of Southern Africa* (1840) is public-domain art. **[sourced]**
- **James Backhouse**, *A Narrative of a Visit to the Mauritius and South Africa* (1844): travelled 1838–39; prices, wages, quaggas, Colesberg and Thaba Nchu.
- **Andrew Steedman**, *Wanderings and Adventures in the Interior of Southern Africa* (1835): the Graham's Town market register of 1831–32 and Albany wages.
- **Thomas Pringle**, *Narrative of a Residence in South Africa* (1835).

## 4. Material culture: wagon, guns, food, money

- **Kakebeenwa** ("jawbone wagon", named for its shape). Oxen were yoked in pairs with a wooden **juk** (yoke) held by a **jukskei** (yoke pin). **[sourced]**
- **Speed:** with herds alongside, about **10 km/day** is typical; oxen walked about 3 mph on rough roads, with a long midday halt. **[sourced]**
- **Oxen per wagon:** 12–16 a span is commonly cited, but no Great-Trek-specific source was found; a source on later dray wagons gives 16–18. The game treats the span as a tuning value.
- **Oldest wagons:** the Msunduzi Museum holds the second-oldest ox wagon in South Africa (1824). **[sourced]**
- **Guns:** *sanna* (the Voortrekker flintlock), *roer* (a heavy long-barrelled hunting gun), *snaphaan* (flintlock), *sterloop* (a popular sanna type with a star mark near the muzzle). **[sourced: DSAE]** Percussion guns came in slowly in the 1840s, but English hunters had them in the 1830s: Steedman's party checked their "copper caps" (1835), and Mzilikazi asked Harris for his "percussion gun". So in 1837 a percussion gun is rare and costly, an English-side luxury at most. **[sourced]**
- **Food:** beskuit (rusks), biltong, dried peaches, coffee, sugar. Women managed provisions. Wagons carried medicine chests and beds, and chicken coops hung underneath. **[sourced, secondary]**
- **Money:** from 1825, 1 rixdollar = 1s 6d, so **£1 ≈ 13⅓ Rds**. Sterling was the official currency by the 1830s, but people still reckoned in rixdollars. Harris gives the same rate in passing: "one hundred Rix dollars, equal to about seven pounds sterling". 1 Rd = 8 skellings (schellingen); 1 skelling = 6 stuivers. **[sourced]**
- **The Dictionary of South African English (dsae.co.za)** is the best tool for checking period words.

## 5. The route and distances

### 5.1 The main road north

Straight-line distances between the modern places, worked out from their coordinates. Ox-wagon tracks wound around hills and between water points, so the distance on the ground is taken as **×1.25–1.3** of the straight line **[estimate]**.

| Leg | Straight line | On the ground (×1.25) |
|---|---|---|
| Grahamstown → Cradock | 95 mi | ~120 mi |
| Cradock → Colesberg | 104 mi | ~130 mi |
| Colesberg → Orange drift (Norvalspont area) | 22 mi | ~28 mi |
| Orange drift → Thaba Nchu | 129 mi | ~160 mi |
| (detour via Philippolis) | | +~23 mi |
| Thaba Nchu → Vet River (Winburg area) | 49 mi | ~60 mi |
| Vet River → Sand River (Ventersburg area) | 31 mi | ~40 mi |
| Sand River → Vaal drift (Parys area) | 84 mi | ~105 mi |
| Vaal drift → Mooi River (Potchefstroom) | 25 mi | ~30 mi |
| **Total** | **539 mi** | **~675 mi** |

The modern places stand in for 1837 crossing points; which drift a given party used varied.

### 5.2 Checking against history

The Uys party received the Bible at Grahamstown in April 1837 **[sourced]** and reached the Sand River laager on 29 June 1837 **[sourced: Wikipedia, Piet Uys]**. That is about 87 days for ~540 mi on the ground, or **~6.2 mi/day overall including rest days**. This matches the sourced "about 10 km/day with herds". A full trip to the Mooi River is then ~100–110 days, arriving in **July**, in the Highveld winter.

### 5.3 Other parties' crossings: the eastern road

Retief's group crossed the Orange at **Buffelsvlei** in March 1837 **[sourced, Afrikaans Retief biography; verify]**. Buffelsvlei was the farm where Aliwal North was later built. Sanddrift, about 18 km upstream of Aliwal North, is shown crossing "the Great River" on a Voortrekker Monument panel **[sourced]**. The trekkers called the Orange the *Groot Rivier* (Great River) **[sourced]**. So besides the western road by Cradock and Colesberg, a real eastern road ran by the Tarka and the Stormberg to Buffelsvlei and the Caledon.

### 7.6 Beyond the Colony: barter

What Harris saw on the road north in 1836–37 **[sourced: Harris, *Wild Sports*, 1839]**:
- Beyond the Colony "no circulating medium exists ... transactions are conducted exclusively by barter". He stocked up on beads, buttons, brass wire, trinkets, snuff and tobacco.
- **Tobacco**, in twisted ropes, was "the current coin of the realm": a roll was a gift for a ruler, an inch a gift for a commoner. Among the Batswana, though, tobacco had lost value because people now grew it, and **beads** were the usual medium.
- **Gunpowder was contraband** beyond the frontier. Harris could not trade it for horses, though "a single pound would have given us the choice of the stud", because he risked **twelve months' imprisonment** back in the Colony.

## 9. Rates for realistic odds

| Rate | Figure | Source | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Infant mortality | ~160–196 per 1,000 births (girls/boys), probably **higher** because deaths were under-recorded | Mamre mission births 1837–46 (UCT thesis) | a Khoikhoi mission community, not trekkers; the best period figure found |
| Settler lifespans 1652–1948 | 401,602 records | Stellenbosch working paper (2012) | figures for the 1830s not yet read |
| Deaths on Tregardt's trek | about half (26–27 of 52–53) | **[sourced]** | mostly malaria on the far northern road; **not** a fair rate for the Highveld |
| Trekker deaths at Vegkop | 2 | **[sourced]** | shows a laager's protective effect |
| Ox losses on a hard, fast trip | Harris: "dreadful mortality", partly from "the rapidity of our march" | **[sourced]** | supports hard pace killing oxen; no rate given |
| Horse deaths | outbreaks with "extremely high mortality" every year or two in Graaff-Reinet, 1819–29; ~40% of the Cape's horses died in 1854–55 | **[sourced]** | horse-sickness is a real seasonal risk |

- **Child deaths:** from ~180 per 1,000 in the first year and lower after, converted to a daily rate; over a 100-day trip that gives a few-percent chance per young child **[estimate from sources]**.
- **Adult illness and accident rates** are **[invented]**, tuned so that on Normal a family of 8–10 usually loses no one or one person over ~100 days on the Highveld road. That seems plausible outside the violent episodes, but it is a judgment call, and the history notes say so.

## 10. Facts checked

### 10.1 The facts table

| Claim | Result |
|---|---|
| Quagga on the route in 1837 | **[sourced]** Backhouse, June 1839, Colesberg district: "A troop of Quaggas, with their foals, were near us at sunrise", along with springbok and gnus. Extinct in the wild by c. 1878; the last captive one died in Amsterdam in 1883. |
| Horse-sickness in summer | **[sourced]** outbreaks mainly in late summer and autumn (modern record); repeated outbreaks in the 1820s (Perry 1830). People then blamed "the night air" in "a season of general malaria". |
| Lungsickness | **[sourced]** first found at Mossel Bay in Sept 1853 (some sources say 1854). It must not appear in an 1837 game. |
| Ordinance 50 (1828) | **[sourced]** removed pass rules for Khoikhoi and other free people of colour. It did **not** cover Africans (Ordinance 49 governed them), so a servant who was Mfengu or Xhosa had fewer rights. |
| Punishment Act 1836 | **[sourced]** 6 & 7 Will. IV c. 57, 13 Aug 1836. Colonial courts could try British subjects for offences "in any part of South Africa" up to **25° S** (EB1911, "South Africa"; Davenport 1987, quoted by the O'Malley archive: jurisdiction "but not sovereignty"). Meant to protect African peoples from settlers; in practice hardly enforced against the trekkers. The Mooi River (~26.7° S) lies inside the line. The British did not stop the trek at the border: their authority was legal and political pressure, not checkpoints. |
| Thaba Nchu mission | **[sourced]** the Barolong and Wesleyan missionaries (Archbell) moved there from Platberg in 1833. |
| Sabbath rest on trek | **[sourced, one party]** Tregardt refused Sunday work because "we anger God by it", but an older man in his party mended a wheel on a Sunday, since people believed Sunday work saved a weekday. Sunday rest was a matter of conscience and varied between families. |
| Slavery and apprenticeship | **[sourced]** slavery ended in law at the Cape on **1 December 1834**; the freed people were bound to their former owners as unpaid "apprentices" until **1 December 1838** (Iziko Slave Lodge; Ekama et al., AEHN working paper 53, 2020). So in April 1837 a formerly enslaved woman would still be an apprentice, travelling with a family only as an apprentice of her former owner, or if she had bought her freedom. |
| Orange River in flood | partly: Potgieter's crossing (Dec 1835) was in flood, with stock swimming and wagons on willow-log rafts; Harris needed raised platforms inside his wagons to keep goods dry. **The seasonal pattern itself is [recall, verify].** |
| Dysentery as a major killer | plausible but not directly sourced. **Malaria** (Tregardt, Delagoa Bay 1838) and **tsetse/nagana** (cattle) are well documented. The game keeps "the flux" as an honest, unsourced homage to Oregon Trail, and says so. |

### 10.2 Tregardt's diary: daily life

The 1938 edition covers Tregardt's party in the far north in 1836–38. Beyond the Far North road itself (section 16), it is used for **daily life**:
- **Sunday:** see section 10.1. Tregardt kept it; others did not always.
- **Animals:** horses died of "de paarde siekte" (horse-sickness); cattle died of a "stop siekte"; he counted his cattle often (454 head in Sept 1837).
- **Powder and lead:** by 1837–38 the party's powder was down to about one keg and they melted tin and copper for bullets (Preller's introduction).
- **Daily work:** wheel bands burnt and reset, hides pegged out, soap boiled, sheep slaughtered, hunting for eland and quagga, lost cattle searched for.
- **Births and deaths** are recorded plainly (twins born and one dying days later, Oct 1836). The game keeps such things in text and non-graphic.
- Daily distances are given in hours, not miles, and were not extracted.

### 10.3 Churches along the road (checked 2026-10-12)

- **Graham's Town:** the Wesleyan chapel was dedicated on 10 Nov 1822, and Wesley Chapel built in 1832 (SAHRIS). St George's (Anglican) opened in 1830 and was consecrated in 1850.
- **Cradock:** Dutch Reformed congregation 1818; first church built 1821–24 on the present site.
- **Colesberg:** congregation 1826; foundation stone 29 Nov 1830. When the building was finished was not found **[conflict]**.
- **Thaba Nchu:** church and mission house re-erected after the 1833 move (SAHRIS); Backhouse saw a T-shaped brick chapel in July 1839.
- **The trekker laager:** no church; services were held in the open, under an awning or in a tent. The Kerkspruit church near Thaba Nchu (Oct 1837) was the first Communion north of the Orange.

### 10.4 Other checks

- **72nd Highlanders** in the 1834–35 war: they built Forts Beresford, Murray and Coke and garrisoned Fort Wellington, then went back to Cape Town late in 1835 (Spink medal lot description). Fort Peddie was named after their Lt-Col John Peddie.
- **The Magaliesberg** was formerly called the Cashan Mountains.

## 11. Peoples and other actors

The people the trekkers met are written as named actors with their own reasons, never as generic encounters. The model is *When Rivers Were Trails* (`design.md` section 22).

| People | Leader (1836–38) | Where they come in |
|---|---|---|
| **Barolong** | **Moroka**, at Thaba Nchu | allies after Vegkop; sent oxen to bring the stranded trek in; the Wesleyan mission |
| **Griqua** | at Philippolis | lived around the Orange River and Philippolis; allies at Mosega in 1837 |
| **Basotho** | **Moshoeshoe** | the Caledon valley |
| **Ndebele** | **Mzilikazi** | held the land north of the Vaal until driven north in 1837 (Mosega, then the Marico campaign) |
| **Zulu kingdom** | **Dingane**, later Mpande | Natal (section 17) |
| **Gaza** | **Soshangane** (Manukosi) | the power to the east of the far north (section 16.4) |
| **Pedi**, **Venda**, **Tsonga traders**, **the Portuguese** | Sekwati, Ramabulana, Capt. Gamitto | the Far North road (section 16.4) |
| **Xhosa** | on the Eastern Cape frontier | the frontier wars behind both sides' stories |
| **Port Natal English traders** | the Biggar family, Dick King | took part in the 1838–42 events; English speakers inside the Trek story |

- **The Mfecane debate.** The idea that the interior was "empty" because of Zulu-driven wars was used to justify settler land claims. Julian Cobbing (1988) challenged it, and it has been debated since.
- **The Sixth Frontier War (Dec 1834–1836)** started over the Kat River lands. King Hintsa was killed in 1835 while held under a truce. Queen Adelaide Province was annexed in May 1835, and the annexation was reversed by Glenelg (Dec 1835–Dec 1836); many frontier farmers saw that reversal as a betrayal. **[sourced]** The game shows the fear and the raids the settlers lived through, and also what drove Xhosa communities: land taken in the frontier wars, the killing of Hintsa, the annexation and its reversal.
- **English speakers on the Trek** were rare. No good source lists them; this is a research gap. Andrew Smith (English) scouted Natal in 1832.
- **The 1820 Settlers' Bible for the Uys trek (April 1837).** The British settlers of Grahamstown, led by Thomas Philipps and W.R. Thompson, gave a large Dutch Bible to the departing Uys party. It is now displayed at the Voortrekker Monument. **[sourced]** This is the game's opening scene, where both sides meet.
- **Servants and companions.** Large numbers of Khoikhoi, formerly enslaved and Black servants (*agterryers*, herders, voorlopers) travelled with the trekkers; one estimate puts them roughly equal in number to the Boers (section 2).

## 12. How the families came to be in South Africa

All playable families are fictional, written over the real history below. Their stories are in `data/locale/en/families.json`.

### 12.1 The Dutch side: background

- **1652:** the Dutch East India Company (VOC) sent Jan van Riebeeck to build a fort and gardens at Table Bay to supply its ships: a supply station, not a colony.
- **1657:** the Company let some of its men leave service and farm as *free burghers* along the Liesbeeck River. The supply station became a colony.
- **From 1688:** French Huguenot refugees arrived, about one-sixth of the free burghers, mostly at Drakenstein. Many German soldiers and sailors of the Company also settled and married into Dutch families.
- **1707:** Hendrik Biebouw (Bibault in some sources) said "'K ben een Africaander", the earliest known claim to be an African-born colonist.
- **1700s:** the *trekboers* moved north and east with their herds, past the Company's limits. Graaff-Reinet was founded in 1786 to bring them back under control. By 1800 they had reached the Great Fish River, and frontier wars with the Xhosa had begun. Khoikhoi and San lost land and were drawn into farm labour. Many colonists held enslaved people.
- **1795 and 1806:** Britain took the Cape. Slagtersnek (1815) left a bitter memory. Ordinance 50 (1828) and emancipation (1834) changed farm labour. The Sixth Frontier War (1834–35) and the giving back of Queen Adelaide Province (1836) left frontier farmers feeling unprotected.

### 12.2 The English side: background

- **1820:** about 4,000–4,500 British settlers came in about 60 parties on some 21–28 ships, landing at Algoa Bay from **about 9–10 April 1820** (the *Chapman* first, then the *Nautilus* and others). They were placed in the Zuurveld, renamed **Albany**, as a buffer on the frontier.
- **1820s:** wheat crops failed again and again with **rust** (1820–23 and worse in 1826). Families lived on maize, pumpkins and potatoes, and many moved to Graham's Town as artisans and traders. Labour and craftsmen were in short supply. The frontier trade in hides, ivory, tallow and butter grew quickly.
- **1834–35:** the Sixth Frontier War hit Albany hard.
- Most 1820 Settlers did **not** join the Trek. The 1820 Settler story runs on in the Eastern Cape into the 1850s (the cattle-killing famine of 1856–57 and so on), so the game keeps to 1837 and never mixes those later events in.

## 14. Event material from the travellers

From Harris, on the same road in 1836–37 **[sourced: Harris, *Wild Sports*, 1839]**:
1. **Wagon upset near Graham's Town:** the baggage wagon fell into a deep hole by the road; the upper works smashed, the goods mostly safe.
2. **Quicksand:** his horse sank in quicksand crossing the Little Fish River and nearly drowned.
3. **Horses go home:** two horses vanished in the night, thought to have gone back to the grazing where they were bred.
4. **Careless herders:** while the herders dozed, the cattle strayed; two oxen died and the rest lost condition.
5. **Oxen dropping:** "three more of our oxen dropped down on the road" near the colonial border.
6. **Mirage:** thirsty oxen ran for a "lake" that was only salt crust on the ground.
7. **The Orange crossing:** the water was so deep they built raised platforms inside the wagons to keep the goods dry.
8. **Thirst:** oxen bellowing for water after a dry march; a stampede into a muddy pool.
9. **Night thieves:** hyenas, drawn by the smell of mutton, ate gear left out.
10. **Dogs' paws:** the dogs' feet were blistered raw by the hot ground and were treated with the tar-and-fat axle grease; the dogs came and held out a paw for more.
11. **Storm:** reeds thrashing in a storm frightened the cattle so that "they thrice broke loose".
12. **News on the road:** reports that the wagons of a hunting farmer named Erasmus had been captured on the Vaal River, before Vegkop. (A real person and event: used only as news, framed carefully.)
13. **Gunpowder bargain:** people would sell horses only for gunpowder, which was illegal to trade (section 7.6).
14. **Predators at night:** hyenas, wild dogs and lions taking stock at night appear in general terms.

From Backhouse (1838–39): a **troop of quaggas with foals at sunrise**; **oxen straying overnight after smelling grass far off**; Boers who **kept their "old price"** while others raised theirs.

- **Natal leaves at the Vet River.** There the gathered trekkers adopted the nine Vet River articles on **6 June 1837** (140 men present) and chose **Port Natal and Natal** as their goal **[sourced: LitNet]**. Potgieter was against going to Natal and stayed on the Highveld **[sourced: Wikipedia, Potgieter]**.
- **The Far North leaves at the Vaal drift.** Potgieter rode north from the trek at the Sand River to the Soutpansberg in mid-1836 to find Tregardt, so the road north from the Sand and Vaal was known **[sourced: Preller's introduction]**.
- **Both forks fit the calendar.** A party leaving Grahamstown in April 1837 reaches the Vet River in late June, when the trekkers there chose Natal, and the far north in the spring, so it meets the **lowveld in the rainy season**, as Tregardt did. That is the deadliest time.

## 16. The Far North (Tregardt and Van Rensburg)

Main source: Gustav Preller's introduction to the 1938 edition of Tregardt's diary (DBNL, pp. LXXVII–XCV), which draws on the diary, Carolus Trichardt's later account, and Francis Owen's letters. Facts below are from it unless marked.

### 16.1 Before 1837

- **Two small parties went north in 1835–36.** Tregardt had 9 families. Van Rensburg's party joined them for a while. They split over Van Rensburg's use of ammunition hunting ivory **[sourced: Wikipedia]**; another account says Van Rensburg went ahead to scout a route to the coast **[conflict]**.
- **Van Rensburg's party, 49 people, was killed** near the Limpopo / on the way to Inhambane in mid-1836 **[sourced; conflict on the details]**. The month is given as June or July; the attackers are named as Soshangane's (Manukosi's) Gaza force, or as Tsonga; two children are said to have been spared.
- **Potgieter rode north to visit Tregardt** at the Soutpansberg in mid-1836. He went past the Limpopo, about 80 miles, and turned back on 17 Aug 1836. They agreed Tregardt would wait for the others. **They never met again**: Vegkop and the war with the Ndebele kept Potgieter in the south.
- **Tregardt waited at the Soutpansberg for over a year**, with houses, a smithy, gardens, a water furrow and a school of **21 children** under the old teacher **Daniel Pfeffer** **[sourced]**.

### 16.2 The road to Delagoa Bay, 1837–38

- **Trade with the coast:** **Tsonga traders (the diary's "Magwambas") came up from Delagoa Bay in the winter**, across the lowveld, with trade goods **[sourced]**. They knew which season was safe. A trader, Waai-Waai, offered in May 1837 to fetch goods from the Bay.
- **The Portuguese commandant sent two soldiers** to guide them (Aug 1837). Tregardt hesitated, then went, because the Ndebele were close to the south, the Van Rensburgs had been killed to the east, and the trekkers' powder was down to one keg and they were melting tin and copper for bullets **[sourced]**. Carolus later said: *"we go through to Delagoa Bay, and the place was death! … I said to my father, let us make a good fort and stay here."*
- **Left on 23 Aug 1837.** They went west and south first to avoid **the fly**: local people warned that the direct road east would bring them into it, as it had the Pretorius party (section 16.3). They passed Chuniespoort and reached the **Olifants River on 2 Oct 1837**.
- **Sekwati (Pedi) warned that the mountains could not be crossed with wagons.** Tregardt didn't believe him and went anyway **[sourced]**.
- **The escarpment descent:** **8 Nov 1837 to 25 Jan 1838**, two months and eighteen days, in heavy rain and mist **[sourced]**.
  - The men nearly gave up and talked of leaving the wagons and walking. The women objected, and Tregardt wrote on 9 Nov that they were glad to hear a road could be made.
  - **On steep slopes they took off the back wheels and tied them on a sledge behind**, protected the axle, and tied the rear oxen's tails to the front axle so they would not fall. Going up took **18 oxen to a wagon** **[sourced, Carolus Trichardt]**.
  - **Jan Pretorius took his wagons apart and carried them down piece by piece** **[sourced]**: a documented case on a steep slope, not a cliff.
- **The lowveld in Feb–Mar 1838, "the unhealthiest months of the year"** **[sourced]**.
  - Cattle died of the "bos-siekte" (bush sickness, the fly): **479 cattle on 23 Mar**, still many on 26 Mar, then most dead within weeks of arrival. In 1836 "fly-disease" struck the pioneers' herds; this is the first record of tsetse in the north of South Africa **[sourced: Kappmeier 1998]**.
  - Local people stole cattle and attacked at night. The wagons laagered every night again.
  - A Portuguese officer met them at the Komati. The ivory and heavy goods went down the river by boat.
- **Arrived at Lourenço Marques, 3 pm on 13 Apr 1838, with 7 wagons** (2 left behind). The place was a small earth fort on a sand ridge, beside a permanent swamp. They fired a salute; no one answered. Then **they had to give up their guns** to the fort, because the Portuguese were afraid of them. The commandant who had sent for them had died; Captain António Gamitto received them kindly **[sourced]**.
- **Fever:** four days after arrival Tregardt's wife Martha, three children and Pfeffer fell ill. Pfeffer died on 21 Apr, and others followed through April. **Martha died on 1 May 1838**, and the diary stops. Carolus's wife and one of their sons died soon after. The Rev. Francis Owen passed through on the *Comet* in May 1838 and wrote that **nobody at the Bay knew any medicine**, and that the Portuguese were very kind. **Tregardt died on 25 Oct 1838.** **[sourced]**
- **Rescue:** news reached Pietermaritzburg in Feb 1839. The *Mazeppa* sailed for the Bay on 25 June 1839 and landed the survivors at **Port Natal on 19 July 1839**: **26 people, most of them children** (25 plus Carolus, who was away exploring the coast) **[sourced]**. Wikipedia says 26 of 52; older summaries say "about 27 of 53 died" **[conflict, small]**. The game says "about half".

### 16.3 The warning nobody heeded

Late in 1836, **Jan Pretorius, Hendrik Botha, Gert Scheepers and Izaak Albach** left Tregardt with their families to look for the Van Rensburgs to the east.
- **Tregardt "had warned them seriously not to go."**
- The season was wrong. **Fever and the fly** struck, and the oxen died until they could go neither forward nor back.
- Scheepers's wife secretly sent a girl back with a letter asking for help. Tregardt sent Venda helpers and spans of oxen.
- **Gert Scheepers died of the fever** on the way back, and later one of his children did too.
- They rejoined on 1 Mar 1837.

This is the model for warnings in the game: someone who knows tells you plainly, and you choose.

## 17. Natal

### 17.1 The road

- **Kerkenberg:** Retief camped with **54 wagons** on **5 Oct 1837**. The trekkers stayed from mid-October to mid-November and **sent parties to find ways down the escarpment** **[sourced]**.
- **Retief rode ahead** on 6 Oct 1837 with about 15 men and 4 wagons, down what is now **Retief's Pass**. He was **welcomed at Port Natal by the traders on 19 Oct 1837** **[sourced]**.
- **2 Nov 1837:** Bantjes rode back to Kerkenberg with word that the trekkers could come down into Natal **[sourced]**.
- The main body went down in **November 1837** and spread out in many small camps along the **Bloukrans and Bushman's (Mtshezi) rivers**, near present Estcourt and Weenen, to wait while Retief went to see the Zulu king Dingane **[sourced]**. This is where most trekkers stopped.
- **How the descent was done:** SA History Online says the hind wheels were removed and heavy branches tied securely underneath, which protected the axles and acted as a brake **[sourced, general: no party, date or pass named]**. That matches Tregardt's method on the northern escarpment (section 16.2). The wagons were **not** dismantled and carried down vertical cliffs, a common exaggeration.
- **Port Natal in 1837:** English traders, about **50 settlers in 1835** **[sourced]** (1837 numbers unknown); **Allen Gardiner's** mission on the hill above the bay, from 1835 (in 1837 he was given authority over the traders, which they refused) **[sourced]**; the **American mission** (Adams, Grout, Champion), from 1836 **[sourced]**. This is where an English family would feel at home.
- Interpreters in Natal came from Port Natal: English traders and missionaries. Thomas Halstead was Retief's interpreter and was killed with him **[recall, verify]**.

### 17.2 What Grahamstown already knew

- **The Kommissietrek of 1834:** Piet Uys led **21 people** to scout Natal. They visited Port Natal and returned to Uitenhage in Feb 1835 with **favourable reports** that helped start the Trek **[sourced]**. Whether they met Dingane is uncertain **[conflict]**.
- So in Grahamstown in April 1837 the talk is of **good, well-watered land in Natal**: the hopeful rumour, set against later warnings.

### 17.3 After the camps: 1838–43

A trip to Natal ends at the camps or at Port Natal in late 1837. What happened next, told without detail:
- **6 Feb 1838:** Retief and about **100** of his party were killed at Dingane's capital, after the land deed was signed **[sourced]**.
- **17 Feb 1838:** the camps on the Bloukrans and Bushman's rivers were attacked at night. Killed: **282 trekkers** (41 men, 56 women, 185 children) and **about 250 Khoikhoi and Basotho** who travelled with them **[sourced; Pretorius gives other figures]**. Some camps beat off the attack. The town later founded there was named **Weenen, "weeping"** **[sourced]**.
- **April 1838:** Piet Uys and his son Dirkie were killed at **Italeni** **[sourced]**. The Port Natal traders' force under Robert Biggar was defeated at the Tugela on **17 Apr 1838**; few came back. Most people at Port Natal escaped on the ship *Comet* **[sourced; numbers conflict]**. The same *Comet* then came to Delagoa Bay and found Tregardt's party dying.
- **16 Dec 1838:** Blood River / Ncome.
- **1838–43:** the Republic of Natalia. **1843:** Britain annexed Natal, and most trekkers left again for the Highveld **[sourced]**.
- **The Far North survivors** were landed at Port Natal in July 1839.

Both sides belong in the telling: Dingane's reasons (land, guns, the trekkers' victory over the Ndebele) as well as the trekkers' grief, and the 250 servants killed, not only the trekkers. These events are specific and contested; they are never a random encounter.

## 19. Malaria and the fly

- **1837 understanding:** people called it **"the fever"** (*koors*) and blamed the low country, swamps and the rainy season. Nobody linked it to mosquitoes until 1897. The game's text uses the period view and the history note gives the modern one **[sourced: general history; Owen's "a consequence of the climate"]**.
- **Quinine** was isolated in 1820 and in wide use elsewhere by the 1840s **[sourced: Utrecht abstract]**. **No source shows the trekkers had it**, and Owen says nobody at the Bay knew medicine. So the game's stores don't sell it; the medicine chest may ease a fever but not cure it **[invented]**.
- **Risk by place and season [estimate]:** Highveld none; Soutpansberg low (the Scheepers child caught it in the lowveld); lowveld and Delagoa Bay high from November to April and low from May to October. The anchor is the Tregardt party: roughly half the people who reached the Bay in April died within a year.
- **The fly** kills oxen, not people, in the lowveld bush. Cattle sicken over weeks. This is why a party can be stranded, as the Pretorius party was.
- **Horse-sickness** (section 10.1) fits the north as well.

The game plays public-domain tunes in a retro tracker style (`data/music.json`, `src/ui/music.c`):
- *Old Hundredth*, the Genevan Psalter tune of c. 1551 (Psalm 134 in the Genevan and Dutch psalters), for the title, church and the ending. Cape congregations went on singing from Datheen's psalm book and the Statenberyming (both set to the Genevan tunes) after the Netherlands moved on, and the trekkers carried their psalm and hymn books inland (NWU repository). **[sourced in general; that this tune was sung on the trek is likely but not shown]**
- *Tallis' Canon* (c. 1567), the English evening hymn, for nights in camp; *St Anne* (Croft, 1708, "O God, our help in ages past") at graves; *O Waly Waly*, a traditional English and Scots folk tune, in towns. Tunes the English settlers of 1820 could have known **[general knowledge; not tied to a specific source]**.

## 22. Common errors to avoid

Claims that turn up in popular retellings and AI summaries of the Trek, and what the sources say instead:

| Claim | What the sources say |
|---|---|
| The trekker musket was a *baviansboud* | Not a period term that could be verified. Use **sanna**, **roer**, **snaphaan** or **sterloop** (section 4). |
| £1 = 1.5 Rds, or prices mixing £ and Rds freely | From 1825, 1 Rd = 1s 6d, so **£1 ≈ 13⅓ Rds** (section 4). |
| Mixed Dutch and Afrikaans spellings (*jager*/*jagter*, *Grens-Smit*) | Pick one: the game uses period Dutch words in English text, spelled consistently. |
| Wagons dismantled and carried down "vertical cliffs" in the Drakensberg | Exaggerated. The usual method was the hind wheels off and branches lashed underneath as a brake. Pretorius did take wagons apart on a steep slope in the north (sections 16.2, 17.1). |
| Kuruman as a trading stop on the trek | Kuruman (Moffat's LMS mission) is far west of the trek routes. Use Thaba Nchu, Philippolis or Platberg. |
| A Griqua guide translating with a Zulu patrol | The Griqua lived around the Orange, far from Zululand. Interpreters in Natal came from Port Natal (section 17.1). |
| British patrols "confiscating weapons" at the border | No source. The British largely did not physically stop the trek; the Punishment Act claimed legal jurisdiction (section 10.1). |
| A "70–75% success rate" for the trek | No source. Use documented episodes instead (Tregardt: about half died). |
| Dysentery as the great killer | Not directly sourced; malaria and nagana are (section 10.1). |
| 1850s events (the cattle-killing famine, missionaries arriving in Natal) in an 1830s trek | Different decades and a different story (section 12.2). |

## 23. Open questions and gaps

- Shop prices of gunpowder, lead, coffee, sugar, tea, beads, cloth, iron and medicine (section 7.4). Places to look: the 1838 *Graham's Town Journal* market column (reproduced in the Huntley Street thesis, Table 2.4, behind a login on Academia); Greig's *South African Almanack* and the Cape Almanacs of 1836–38; the Graaff-Reinet price series in Cilliers (2023).
- isiXhosa names, to be checked with a speaker or a period register (section 13.3).
- A second reader for the family stories and the history notes, for tone and accuracy.
- The Natal descent: a first-hand account of the method, and which passes were used when.
- Fuller (1932), *Louis Trichardt's Trek across the Drakensberg* (Van Riebeeck Society 13), with maps of the Far North route: not found online.
- The Kommissietrek report itself: only secondary summaries.
- Venda dates: sources disagree on when Ramabulana gave the land (1836 or 1837).
- The seasonal flooding pattern of the Orange (section 10.1).
- English speakers on the Trek (section 11).
